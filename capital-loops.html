Physical AI — Circular Capital
Companion note · September 2026

The Money Goes in a Circle

Who funds physical AI, who supplies it, and who buys from it are increasingly the same parties. This note maps four loops, then asks which are ordinary ecosystem building and which make a valuation hard to read.
V1-00 · September 2026 · companion to the Physical AI Market Map
Sources: NVIDIA and portfolio-company announcements, TechCrunch, Bloomberg, Forbes, Korea Economic Daily, The Robot Report, Dealroom
Across AI generally, roughly $800B of arrangements have been described as circular — vendors investing in customers who spend the money back on the vendor's product. Physical AI has its own version, and it is tighter, because the same industrial groups can be investor, component supplier and end customer at once.

Why this shape appears

It is not usually a conspiracy. Early capital-intensive markets have a genuine bootstrapping problem: the supplier needs a customer to justify capacity, the customer needs a supplier to exist at all, and neither can wait for the other. Equity is how that gets bridged. Intel Capital and Qualcomm Ventures ran this playbook for decades and built real ecosystems.

The reason to map it anyway is narrower. When an investor is also a supplier and a customer, the price it pays for equity stops being an independent read on the company's worth. That matters for a market map whose valuation column is the most-quoted thing on it.

Four loops

1 · The compute loopNVIDIA
The tightest and most documented of the four.
Invests
NVIDIA takes equity in Figure (Series B at $2.6B, again in the Series C at $39B), Wayve ($1.05B round, with a further ~$500M reported), Skild AI and Field AI.
They build on NVIDIA
Those companies train on Isaac Lab and Cosmos, and ship on Jetson. Roughly a dozen ecosystem partners — ABB, AgiBot, Agility, FANUC, KUKA, Universal Robots, World Labs, Yaskawa — are publicly built on the same stack.
Revenue returns
Compute and licence spend flows back to NVIDIA, and the ecosystem announcements support the platform narrative that underwrites its own multiple.
Closing the loopSkild's model, trained on NVIDIA software and funded partly by NVIDIA, runs the Foxconn assembly lines in Houston that build NVIDIA's own Blackwell servers. Investor, toolchain, customer and end market are the same company.
Scale: NVIDIA is reported to be working through a deal pipeline above $750B, including ~$500B of financing partnerships with BlackRock and Apollo that extend credit for GPU purchases. Jensen Huang has publicly rejected the "circular financing" characterisation, calling it a virtuous cycle rather than manufactured demand.
2 · The component loopSchaeffler · Bosch
The clearest case of a supplier funding its own demand.
Invests
Schaeffler and Bosch both take part in Neura Robotics' Series C and Humanoid's Series A. Schaeffler separately partners with Leju Robotics in China.
Supplies them
Schaeffler provides drivetrain components to Agility and to its portfolio companies — the actuation layer these robots cannot be built without.
Then buys the output
Schaeffler has an order for 1,000+ robots from Humanoid, a deal reported as implying 100,000 units by 2031, destined for its own plants.
Closing the loopOne company is simultaneously shareholder, component vendor and anchor customer. Every leg is individually rational; together they mean Humanoid's order book and Schaeffler's equity mark are not independent of each other.
Note: this is also how the automotive tier-one system has always worked — co-development with an equity stake is normal there. The difference is that the robot's valuation is being set in venture markets, not negotiated as a supply contract.
3 · The conglomerate loopHyundai · Samsung · LG
Where the group owns the maker, the supply chain and the factory.
Owns the maker
Hyundai moved to full ownership of Boston Dynamics via the SoftBank buyout. Samsung raised its Rainbow Robotics stake to ~35% (₩267B, ~$181M) to become largest shareholder. LG took a $60M position in Bear Robotics.
Directs the supply chain
Hyundai has mobilised roughly 200 tier-one suppliers to build a domestic humanoid component base, asking them for business plans in the category.
Deploys into itself
Hyundai plans about 25,000 Atlas units across its own factories from 2028 — the group is the manufacturer, the supplier network and the customer.
Closing the loopVertical integration at chaebol scale. It removes the market test entirely: internal deployment volume proves the group's commitment, not that a third party would buy at that price.
Why it still matters: internal deployment is real revenue and real operating data — the embodied-data-density argument applies. But an internal transfer price is not a market price, and 25,000 units bought from yourself is a weaker demand signal than 25,000 sold to strangers.
4 · The strategic syndicateNeura Series C
Not a loop so much as the absence of an arm's-length buyer.

Neura Robotics' Series C — up to $1.4B, milestone-contingent — was assembled from Tether, NVIDIA, Amazon, Qualcomm, Bosch, Schaeffler, the European Investment Bank and a Qatari sovereign investor. Read the list again: a stablecoin issuer, two chip vendors, a cloud and logistics operator, two component suppliers, a development bank and a sovereign. There is no classic venture lead setting the price.

Reporting on the sector's largest 2025–26 rounds describes the same pattern generally: the biggest cheques were assembled by corporate strategic investors acting in coordinated syndicates rather than priced by financial investors. Dealroom counted about $8.7B of humanoid venture funding in 2026 to late July.

What this does and does not mean

The dot-com comparison is the one critics reach for: Nortel and Lucent lent customers the money to buy switching gear, and the revenue evaporated when the credit did. It is a fair analogy for the mechanism, and an unfair one for the conclusion — vendor financing did not make fibre worthless, it made the reported demand curve wrong.

The defensible reading is narrower and more useful than either cheerleading or alarm. Circular structure is not evidence that the technology is fake. It is evidence that the price signal is degraded. Where investor, supplier and customer are the same party, a headline valuation and an order book are corroborating each other rather than independently confirming anything.

Four questions that separate the healthy from the hollow

1Would the customer buy at the same volume absent the equity stake? Schaeffler ordering robots it also part-owns is a weaker signal than a competitor of Schaeffler ordering them.
2Is the back-flow at market terms and separately disclosed? Ecosystem investment survives scrutiny when the revenue is broken out. It does not when it is buried in a segment line.
3Did a financial investor set the price, or only strategics? A round with no arm's-length lead has no independent pricing anchor — the Neura pattern.
4Does the company have revenue from outside the loop? This is the one that resolves it. External customers are the only proof the demand is not manufactured.

What would change this reading

  • A major humanoid or foundation-model company disclosing revenue split by strategic-investor versus independent customer. Nobody currently does, and that absence is itself informative.
  • A priced round in the sector led by a financial investor with no commercial relationship to the company, at or above the last strategic mark.
  • Schaeffler's Humanoid order converting to repeat volume from unaffiliated buyers, which would convert an anchor commitment into a market.
  • Conversely: a down round at a company whose last mark was set entirely by its own suppliers and customers would confirm the pricing concern quickly and publicly.
Scope and limits

This note maps publicly announced investments, partnerships and orders. Deal terms are mostly undisclosed, so it can show that a relationship exists but not what was paid or on what terms. Several figures — the NVIDIA pipeline, the additional Wayve tranche, the Neura milestone conditions — are reported rather than confirmed, and are described as such above.

Nothing here asserts wrongdoing. Strategic investment is ordinary, frequently value-additive, and in a capital-intensive early market often the only way a supply chain gets built at all. The argument is about how much a valuation tells you, not about anyone's conduct.
