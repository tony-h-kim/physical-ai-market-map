Deliberately out of scope

Surgical and medical robotics (Intuitive Surgical, CMR Surgical), consumer and domestic robots (iRobot, Matic, Roborock), food service (Miso Robotics) and laboratory automation (Ginkgo, Emerald Cloud) remain outside this map.

The remaining exclusions share the same three-plane stack and would slot in at A6–A12 and C1–C5 without altering the taxonomy. That the structure extends to them without modification is itself a test of whether it holds. Surgical robotics in particular is a defensible future addition: Intuitive alone generates more revenue than most of this map combined.

Most published landscapes split physical AI into hardware and software, then merge simulation, world models and robot policies into a single bucket. Both moves hide where value actually accrues.

Why not hardware versus software

NVIDIA is silicon and Isaac, Cosmos and GR00T. Figure is hardware and Helix 2. Tesla, Unitree, 1X, Anduril and Waymo are all both. A hardware/software split places the fifteen most important companies in two boxes each, and asserts a separation that does not yet exist in this market. It is not mutually exclusive — and it is not collectively exhaustive either, because it has nowhere to put fleet orchestration, safety certification or systems integration.

The instinct is right; the altitude is wrong. Hardware-weighting is real, but it is a gradient across layers, not a wall down the middle.

Three planes, twenty-five layers

Plane A is broadly hardware, Plane B broadly software, and Plane C is the operational layer that is neither — and that determines whether anything ships.

Why B4, B5 and B6 are three layers and not one

They are routinely collapsed into "simulation and models". They are three different technical bets, with different customers and different failure modes.

  • B4 physics simulation solves dynamics from specified equations. You author the world. Failure mode is the sim-to-real gap.
  • B5 world models learn dynamics generatively and predict future states. You sample the world rather than specify it. Failure mode is hallucinated physics — and per NVIDIA's own finding, when the video prediction fails the action fails with it.
  • B6 robot foundation models output motor commands. They consume B4 and B5 output but sell a policy, not an environment.
  • The honest caveat on B5: the standalone commercial market is thin. Several entries — Cosmos, Genie — are internal tooling inside a larger platform rather than products sold on their own, and are tagged internal on the map. Independently funded pure plays do exist (World Labs at $5B, Decart, Odyssey), which is why the layer is kept separate rather than folded away — but it is the layer most likely to collapse into its neighbours.

The assignment rule — unit of sale, because MECE is a property of the rule, not the boxes

Every company is assigned to one primary layer: the unit of sale — what the customer actually buys. This is deliberately narrower than "where value is captured", which is too elastic to adjudicate — it invites a debate about where the value really sits. Unit of sale is checkable: Waymo’s customer buys a ride, so Waymo sits in C5 no matter how much of the stack it built; Nuro’s customer licenses a driving stack, so Nuro sits in B7.

Secondary positions are recorded as spans and rendered with a dashed border, never as duplicate entries.

Seven companies — Waymo, Tesla, NVIDIA, Anduril, Amazon, Figure, Zoox — are marked full-stack. To be precise about what that does and does not mean: they are still placed at one layer, because the map is a grid and everything has to sit somewhere. The placement is their unit of sale. The mark exists to tell you that the placement is unusually lossy for these seven, and that the spans matter more than the primary layer. It is a warning label on the taxonomy, not an exemption from it.

What Plane C buys you — and what it is not

CB Insights' widely-cited physical AI map covers 70+ companies across 10 categories — all of which sit inside Plane B. It is an excellent map of the intelligence layer, and silent on the other two-thirds of the stack.

Plane C is split for honesty. C1–C3 are genuine technical layers — runtime, fleet operations, certification. C4–C5 are commercial delivery, and they are not technology: systems integration is a low-margin, headcount-heavy professional-services business, and RaaS is an off-balance-sheet financing structure. They earn their place because they own the customer and the operational envelope, but reading them as a software layer would misprice them badly.

That silence matters, because the binding constraint on industrial deployment is not model capability but certifiability against a documented operational envelope. Roland Berger puts Tier 2 reliability at >98% with human exception handling — and that qualifier carries most of the cost.

Assume a 60-second takt and an 8-hour shift (~450 cycles per station), each exception requiring ~4 minutes of human intervention on a coupled line with no buffer. At 98% that is ~9 interventions and ~36 line-minutes, or 7.5% of the shift. At 99.9% — the fixed-automation norm — it is ~0.45 interventions and 1.8 minutes, or 0.4%. Roughly a 19× difference in intervention events. Layer C3 is where that gap is closed or it is not, and it is the least-mapped layer in the industry.

Sensitivity, because the 19× depends on two choices I made: whether Roland Berger's “>98%” means per-cycle task success or system uptime, and whether the line is coupled with no buffer. Change the second and the conclusion softens considerably. In a buffered parallel cell — independent stations, work-in-progress between them, a failed unit simply pulled from rotation — the same 9 exceptions per shift cost throughput roughly in proportion to the failure rate rather than stopping the line, so the penalty falls from ~7.5% of shift time to something closer to 2%. That is the architecture where cheap redundancy substitutes for reliability, and it is why logistics adopted robots a decade before manufacturing did. The reliability argument is an argument about coupled linear lines specifically, not about robots in general.

Arithmetic above is the author's own estimate from Roland Berger's published per-tier reliability targets plus standard takt assumptions — not a sourced figure.

The tier framework's hardest test — and why it survives it

Mining and agriculture appear to falsify this table. Caterpillar's MineStar and Komatsu's FrontRunner have run driverless haul trucks in production mines for over a decade, moving billions of tonnes in open, unstructured terrain. John Deere ships a fully autonomous tractor. That looks like Tier 3–4 autonomy, profitable, today.

It is not a contradiction — it is the strongest available evidence for the argument. A mine haul road is geofenced, single-task, low-speed, on private land with no public-road regulator and no member of the public present. A field is a bounded polygon. These are not general-purpose systems operating in the open world; they are narrow, rigorously documented operational design domains, which is precisely the condition this map argues you need. The lesson is not that Tier 4 arrived early. It is that when you constrain the envelope hard enough, autonomy has been commercially viable in heavy industry for fifteen years — and that is the template, not the humanoid.

Read the two axes together and the map's central tension appears: capital is concentrated in Plane B and Tier 3–4 ambition, while revenue is concentrated in Plane A/C and Tier 1–2 reality — plus the mining and agriculture deployments that most physical AI maps leave out entirely.

Finding 1 — the capital/shipment inversion

Western companies hold the overwhelming majority of disclosed capital. Chinese companies hold the overwhelming majority of units shipped. Chinese firms accounted for over 80% of global humanoid installations in 2025 (Counterpoint), and China took 54% of all industrial robot installations in 2024 — 295,000 of 542,076 units (IFR) — with domestic manufacturers outselling foreign suppliers at home for the first time.

Unitree shipped 5,500+ humanoids in 2025 on roughly CNY 1.7B (~$235M) revenue at a ~$5.9B IPO valuation. Figure, which has shipped a small fraction of that, is valued at $39B. AgiBot reported 5,100+ units and $140M+ revenue and targets $5.1–6.4B. Valuation is being set in one hemisphere and volume in the other. If embodied data density is the durable moat, the capital is on the wrong side of the flywheel.

Finding 2 — the same strategic split recurs in every vertical

In defence, Anduril is vertically integrated (hardware, sensors and Lattice) while Shield AI sells horizontal autonomy that runs on anyone's airframe. In humanoids, Figure integrates while Physical Intelligence and Skild sell the brain to any body. In AV, Tesla and Waymo integrate while Wayve, Waabi and Applied Intuition license the driver or the tooling. This is one strategic question — own the embodiment or own the intelligence — asked independently in five verticals, with both answers funded at scale in each. That cannot hold in all five, which makes it the single most useful place to form a differentiated view.

Where does margin actually pool? The layers commoditise at very different rates, and the current capital allocation implies a view about that which is worth stating explicitly — and disagreeing with.

Three places margin is likelier to pool

C3 · Safety & certification

The most under-discussed moat in the stack. Certification cost amortised across an ecosystem is stickier than any licence, because it is a sunk regulatory asset rather than a technical lead.

NVIDIA's Halos pre-assesses the platform so partners certify only application logic. No third-party certificate has yet been issued, and TÜV SÜD inspection is not certification — so the moat is announced but not yet proven.

B2 · Proprietary deployment data

Cannot be replicated in simulation and cannot be bought. A US defence contractor's operational data is structurally inaccessible to a Chinese OEM — jurisdiction is part of the moat.

The counter-case is serious: NVIDIA's Ego-Scale result pre-trains on 21,000 hours of egocentric human video with zero robot data, then fine-tunes on 4 hours of teleoperation. If that scales, this moat is worth far less than assumed.

A1 · Actuation bottlenecks

Harmonic and strain-wave gearboxes are the clearest structural constraint — capacity cannot be added quickly, unlike electronics. Concentrated in Harmonic Drive, Nabtesco and rising Chinese entrants such as Leaderdrive.

Planetary roller screws are tighter still: narrow supplier base, long lead times, minimal substitution. Goldman's supply-chain-over-OEM call rests here — with erosion risk from Chinese entrants pricing 30–50% below incumbents.

The contrarian read

Capital is flowing hardest into the two layers with the weakest margin durability. That is defensible only if you believe general-purpose capability arrives fast enough for a winner to take a platform position before commoditisation bites — the Android/iPhone bet. If instead deployment stays gated on per-ODD certification, value accrues to whoever owns the operational envelope and the customer relationship, not to whoever owns the best weights. Those two futures imply almost opposite portfolios.

What would change this view

  • A general-purpose platform sustaining >99% on a Tier 3 task for 90 consecutive days without task-specific retraining between changeovers.
  • A humanoid OEM publishing a certified ODD with ISO 25785-1 conformance for an unstructured industrial task, breaching the C3 moat.
  • Demonstrated cross-embodiment transfer from humanoid to purpose-built without retraining, collapsing the A6–A12 morphology question into a deployment-time choice.
  • Sub-$15K humanoid-class hardware achieving 98%+ on a real production line, making redundancy a viable substitute for reliability — though only in parallel architectures, not coupled lines.
  • The dexterity scaling law holding two further orders of magnitude with published sim-to-real transfer at industrial tolerance.
A market map that stops at "who exists" is a directory. This section answers the questions an investor actually asks next: how big is it, who has revenue, when does liquidity arrive, and where would you put money.

What

The cleanest evidence that the market is pricing a binary is not the forecast spread — it is a struck price. Agility Robotics went public via SPAC at roughly $2.5B with the deepest deployment record in humanoids. Figure is marked at $39B with no disclosed revenue. One of those is a number a market agreed to pay; the other is a number a small syndicate agreed to write down. A ~15× gap between them, in the same sub-sector in the same year, is the disagreement made concrete.

The forecast spread is weaker evidence and should be read as such — it tells you analysts disagree about scope and adoption, not that anyone underwrites off ABI versus Roland Berger. Still, for humanoids alone in 2035 the range is $38B to $750B, roughly 20×, and even like-for-like Goldman's $38B against Macquarie's $139B is a 3.7× gap on the same definition in the same year.

When credible forecasts disagree by an order of magnitude, the market is not pricing a growth curve — it is pricing a binary. The disagreement is not really about adoption rates. It is about whether general-purpose capability works at industrial reliability at all. That is the same question the Structure tab answers with reliability economics, arriving from the opposite direction. You are not underwriting a TAM; you are underwriting a technical outcome with a TAM attached.

Every venture-backed humanoid or robot-foundation-model company with materially disclosed revenue is Chinese. That qualifier matters: the incumbents in A7, A11 and A12 — ABB, FANUC, Caterpillar, Komatsu, John Deere, Symbotic — all disclose substantial robotics and autonomy revenue. The point is not that nobody in the West earns money from robots. It is that within the venture-funded frontier this map is mostly about, the revenue is Chinese and the valuations are not. UBTech led humanoid revenue in 2025 at roughly CNY 2.0B (~$280M), selling 1,079 Walker S2 units for CNY 821M (~$115M) of humanoid-segment revenue. Unitree guided H1 2026 revenue of about CNY 1.1B (~$156M), up 36–45% year on year. AgiBot expects roughly CNY 4.0B (~$594M) for 2026. Global humanoid shipments reached 19,100 units in H1 2026, up 272% year on year, with AgiBot overtaking Unitree for the top position and the two together holding close to 80% of Chinese output.

Western leaders are valued on capability narrative. Chinese leaders are valued on shipped units and revenue — and at 9–25× revenue, which is expensive but not unhinged. The Western marks have no denominator at all. That is not automatically wrong: platform bets are supposed to look like this early. But it means the two halves of this map are being underwritten on completely different evidentiary standards, and only one of them can be checked.

Pricing is unsettled even among people who look at the same company

AgiBot's Hong Kong listing target has been reported both as $5.1–6.4B and as HK$156B (~$20B) — a 3× spread on an imminent, disclosed transaction. When the range on a company about to list is that wide, treat every private mark on this map as a soft number.

4 · The incumbents are absent from this map, and that is strange

ABB, FANUC, Yaskawa and KUKA appear only in A7. They hold the installed base — the industry shipped 542,076 industrial robots in 2024 alone, on an operational stock in the millions — plus the service revenue, the distribution and the customer trust that every startup on this map is trying to rent.

They are almost entirely absent from A5, B2 and B5. Historically, industrial-automation incumbents acquire late and expensively rather than build. The Mobileye/Mentee deal suggests the acquisition phase has begun, but it began with a silicon incumbent rather than a robotics one.

The more urgent signal is that the incumbents are already losing their largest market. Chinese domestic manufacturers outsold foreign suppliers inside China for the first time in 2024 — in a market that is 54% of global installations. The incumbent question is not whether they get disrupted by humanoids in 2030. It is that they are being displaced right now, by Chinese industrial-arm makers, in the market that matters most.

6 · What this map still cannot tell you

The residual gaps, stated rather than hidden. Each would change conclusions if resolved.

  • Gross margin per deployed robot. Nobody publishes it. Without it, every RaaS and OEM model on this map is a guess about whether the unit works.
  • Exception-handling headcount. The single number that would settle the reliability argument empirically — and its consistent absence from every vendor disclosure is itself informative.
  • Whether B5 is a layer or a feature. If world models collapse into B6 as a training technique rather than a sold product, one of these twenty-five layers disappears.
  • Real depth of the Chinese component tier. Roughly 30 Chinese companies appear here against a population several times larger. In the half of the market that ships the units, this map is thinnest.
  • Renewal and churn in RaaS. Three-year contracts have not yet cycled. The deployment-moat thesis is untested past first renewal.
How the map was built, what it excludes, and where the data is weakest. Stated because a landscape without a method is a logo collection.

Taxonomy rules

  1. One primary layer per entry, assigned by unit of sale — what the customer buys — not by what the company touches, aspires to, or is valued on.
  2. Product lines can be their own entry where they compete as separate products against separate rivals. Isaac Sim, Cosmos, GR00T and Halos are listed individually because a simulation buyer, a world-model buyer and a certification buyer are different people running different procurements. Those entries are tagged product, excluded from the company count, and removed from the parent's spans so nothing is counted twice. 243 distinct companies, 251 entries.
  3. Ties broken by what the customer buys. Applied Intuition touches models but sells tooling, so B4. Symbotic builds robots but sells an integrated warehouse, so A7 with a span into C4 and C5.
  4. Secondary positions recorded as spans, rendered as dashed borders, never as duplicate entries. Where no layer dominates, the company is marked full-stack instead of being forced.
  5. Geography is headquarters, not manufacturing or market. Several Western OEMs manufacture predominantly in China; that is a supply-chain fact, not a taxonomy fact.
  6. Verticals are tags, not boxes. A company can serve several. Only its layer is exclusive.

Valuation methodology

  • Valuations only — never round sizes. A $1.4B round and a $14B valuation are different facts, and showing both in one column invites misreading.
  • Publicly listed companies show no figure, only a listed tag. Market caps move daily and would date the map within a week.
  • Reported-but-unclosed marks are flagged in amber. 1X at ~$10B and AgiBot at $5.1–6.4B are talks and IPO targets, not completed rounds. Physical Intelligence is shown at its confirmed $5.6B, not the ~$11B reported in negotiation.
  • Blank means undisclosed, not small. PitchBook shows no post-money for Waabi despite a $750M round — a disclosure gap, not an absence of value.
  • Headline marks are not market caps, and should not be read as one. Late-stage private valuations are pre-money headline numbers agreed between a company and a small number of investors. They routinely sit on top of liquidation preferences, participation rights and ratchets that mean common equity is worth materially less than the headline implies. Several rounds on this map also involve strategic or circular structure — compute commitments, supplier equity, or an infrastructure vendor investing in a customer that buys its hardware. Aggregating these figures overstates the sector's real market scale, and does so most in Plane B, where marks are highest and revenue is thinnest.

Known weaknesses

GapEffect
China under-mappedThe most consequential gap. China ships the majority of both humanoid and industrial units, but English-language coverage of its component and integrator tiers is thin. Roughly 30 Chinese companies appear here against a real population several times larger.
Valuation stalenessPrivate marks are disclosed irregularly. In a market repricing this fast, several entries may understate by a full round.
Conflicting sourcesSaronic is reported at both $9.25B and ~$4B; the Crunchbase Series D figure is used. Apptronik's round and valuation figures conflict across trackers; ~$5.3B is the most consistently reported. Helsing appears at both $12B and $18B; the confirmed lower figure is used.
Plane C under-populatedPartly real — the layer is genuinely immature — and partly a coverage gap, since integrators and RaaS operators are often private, unbranded, or embedded inside larger service businesses.
SurvivorshipWound-down and acquired companies (Cruise, Covariant, Kindred) are largely absent, which flatters the sector's apparent success rate.
Full-stack is a judgementSeven companies are marked full-stack. That line is drawn by whether any single layer plausibly accounts for the majority of the offer — defensible, but not mechanical. A stricter reading would mark more; a looser one, fewer.
Incumbent depth in A10Mining and agriculture are represented by their major OEMs and a handful of retrofit startups. The tier-two supplier base in both — and the service contractors who actually run the fleets — is not mapped.

How this differs from the published maps

a16z, CB Insights and the VC landscape posts are organised by category membership — useful for discovery, and silent on where value goes. This map is organised by unit of sale, states its assignment rule, extends beyond the intelligence layer into operations, and attaches a falsifiable view about margin geography.

Being precise about what is and is not original here, because a reader who works it out unaided will discount more than the truth warrants: the company roster is compiled, not discovered. The Capital tab is substantially a restatement of one Crunchbase analysis plus PitchBook round medians — accurate, verified, and not mine. The contributions are four: Plane C as a first-class plane, the unit-of-sale assignment rule published rather than assumed, the margin-geography read, and the deployment-tier framework together with the argument that mining and agriculture confirm rather than falsify it.
