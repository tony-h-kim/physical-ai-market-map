Deliberately out of scope

Surgical and medical robotics (Intuitive Surgical, CMR Surgical), consumer and domestic robots (iRobot, Matic, Roborock), food service (Miso Robotics) and laboratory automation (Ginkgo, Emerald Cloud) remain outside this map.

The remaining exclusions share the same three-plane stack and would slot in at A6–A12 and C1–C5 without altering the taxonomy. That the structure extends to them without modification is itself a test of whether it holds. Surgical robotics in particular is a defensible future addition: Intuitive alone generates more revenue than most of this map combined.

That exclusion costs the margin analysis more than it costs the taxonomy. Value capture argues about where durable margin pools in physical AI while omitting the vertical that has most clearly demonstrated one. Intuitive Surgical built a razor-and-blade model on instruments and service around a certified capital platform, which is close to the structure this map argues will prove durable in C3 and C5. Leaving it out means the strongest available evidence for that argument sits outside the map. Any reader weighing the margin conclusions should treat medical robotics as a supporting case that has been excluded for scope reasons rather than an absent one.

Finding 1 — the capital/shipment inversion

Western companies hold the overwhelming majority of disclosed capital. Chinese companies hold the overwhelming majority of units shipped. Chinese firms accounted for over 80% of global humanoid installations in 2025 Counterpoint, and China took 54% of all industrial robot installations in 2024, 295,000 of 542,076 units IFR, with domestic manufacturers outselling foreign suppliers at home for the first time.

Unitree shipped more than 5,500 humanoids in 2025 Unitree on roughly CNY 1.7B (about $235M) of revenue, at an IPO valuation near $5.9B. Figure has shipped a small fraction of that volume and is marked at $39B. AgiBot reported more than 5,100 units and over $140M of revenue against a listing target of $5.1–6.4B.

Valuation is being set in one hemisphere and volume in the other. The implication depends on what the durable advantage in this market turns out to be. If it is the density of real-world operating data, then capital is concentrated on the side of that trade currently generating less of it. If instead the advantage sits in model capability or in the ability to fund a long pre-revenue build, the allocation looks more defensible. We would not treat the inversion as self-evidently mispriced, but it is the clearest structural asymmetry on the map.

Finding 2 — the same strategic split recurs in every vertical

In defence, Anduril is vertically integrated (hardware, sensors and Lattice) while Shield AI sells horizontal autonomy that runs on anyone's airframe. In humanoids, Figure integrates while Physical Intelligence and Skild sell the brain to any body. In AV, Tesla and Waymo integrate while Wayve, Waabi and Applied Intuition license the driver or the tooling. This is a single strategic question, own the embodiment or own the intelligence, being asked independently in five verticals, with both answers funded at scale in each one. It is unlikely that both approaches prove correct everywhere. That makes this a productive place to form a differentiated view, because the market is currently declining to choose.

Layers in this market commoditise at very different rates. Today's capital allocation implies a particular view about which of them will hold their margin. This section makes that implied view explicit, sets out where we think it is questionable, and states what evidence would change it.

Three places margin is likelier to pool

C3 · Safety & certification

One of the least discussed layers in the stack, and on our reading one of the more defensible. Certification cost, once amortised across an ecosystem, tends to be stickier than a software licence: it is a sunk regulatory asset rather than a technical lead, and a competitor cannot shortcut it by building a better product.

NVIDIA's Halos pre-assesses the platform so that partners certify only their own application logic. The assurance infrastructure here is older than it first appears: NVIDIA's DRIVE AI Systems Inspection Lab was accredited to ISO/IEC 17020 by ANAB in January 2025 for autonomous vehicles NVIDIA, and ANAB expanded that scope to robotics under Halos in June 2026 ANAB. The accreditation covers inspection, and presents itself as a foundation for later certification rather than a replacement for it, so the technical distinction holds: inspection is not certification.

The instructive fact is who built it. The accredited lab belongs to the compute vendor rather than to an independent certification body, and established assessors including TÜV SÜD, TÜV Rheinland and UL Solutions now treat its inspection reports as valid inputs to their own work, with pre-assessed pathways described for named partners. That is a more developed position than "announced". It also points somewhere specific: if the layer we assess as the most defensible on this map is being assembled by the platform that already sells into every other layer, then C3 looks less like a standalone profit pool and more like an extension of NVIDIA's. On current evidence that reading is better supported than a cautious one.

B2 · Proprietary deployment data

Cannot be replicated in simulation and cannot be bought. A US defence contractor's operational data is structurally inaccessible to a Chinese OEM — jurisdiction is part of the moat.

The counter-case is serious: NVIDIA's Ego-Scale result pre-trains on 21,000 hours of egocentric human video with zero robot data, then fine-tunes on 4 hours of teleoperation. If that scales, this moat is worth far less than assumed.

A1 · Actuation bottlenecks

Harmonic and strain-wave gearboxes are the clearest structural constraint — capacity cannot be added quickly, unlike electronics. Concentrated in Harmonic Drive, Nabtesco and rising Chinese entrants such as Leaderdrive.

Planetary roller screws are tighter still: narrow supplier base, long lead times, minimal substitution. Goldman's supply-chain-over-OEM call rests here — with erosion risk from Chinese entrants pricing 30–50% below incumbents.

Reading the allocation against the durability

Capital is flowing hardest into the two layers we assess as having the weakest margin durability. That allocation makes sense under one specific assumption: that general-purpose capability arrives quickly enough for a leader to establish a platform position before price competition sets in. It is the same bet the smartphone platforms won. The alternative is that deployment stays gated on certifying one operating envelope at a time, in which case value accrues to whoever owns that envelope and the customer relationship rather than to whoever trains the best model. The two paths imply close to opposite portfolios, which is why it is worth deciding which one you are underwriting.

What would change this view

  • A general-purpose platform sustaining >99% on a Tier 3 task for 90 consecutive days without task-specific retraining between changeovers.
  • A humanoid OEM publishing a a certified ODD under a finalised mobile-robot safety standard (ISO 25785-1 remains a committee draft ISO, so nothing can yet be certified against it) for an unstructured industrial task, breaching the C3 moat.
  • Demonstrated cross-embodiment transfer from humanoid to purpose-built without retraining, collapsing the A6–A12 morphology question into a deployment-time choice.
  • Sub-$15K humanoid-class hardware achieving 98%+ on a real production line, making redundancy a viable substitute for reliability — though only in parallel architectures, not coupled lines.
  • The dexterity scaling law holding two further orders of magnitude with published sim-to-real transfer at industrial tolerance.
Knowing who exists is the starting point rather than the conclusion. This section works through the questions that follow: how large the market might be, which companies actually earn revenue, where liquidity is arriving, what the incumbents are doing, and what all of that implies for where to allocate.

How to read the spread

Before treating the range as disagreement, it is worth separating out what is simply definitional. The forecasts span different horizons, from 2030 to 2050, and different scopes, from humanoid hardware only to whole-ecosystem revenue. Much of the apparent gap is scope rather than conviction. The sharper evidence sits elsewhere. Agility Robotics has agreed a SPAC merger at roughly $2.5B and has the deepest deployment record in humanoids, while Figure is marked at $39B with no disclosed revenue. One of those is a number a market agreed to pay; the other is a number a small syndicate agreed to write down. A ~15× gap between them, in the same sub-sector in the same year, is the disagreement made concrete.

Read on its own, the forecast spread is weak evidence. It tells you that analysts differ on scope and adoption, not that anyone is underwriting off one house against another. The more instructive comparison is like-for-like: Goldman at $38B against Macquarie at $139B, both for humanoids in 2035, is a 3.7× gap on the same definition in the same year. That narrower figure is the one worth citing.

Both readings point the same way, and neither is really about adoption rates. The open question is whether general-purpose capability reaches industrial reliability at all, which is where the Structure section arrives from a different direction. In practice an investor here is underwriting a technical outcome, with a market size attached to it rather than driving it.

The nearest Western counterexample is worth stating before the claim, because it is the one a sceptical reader will reach for. Agility Robotics disclosed more than $300M in booked orders in its SPAC materials Form 425, alongside roughly 65,000 hours of operation across nine states and named customers including Amazon, GXO, Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada and Schaeffler. Its chief executive was explicit that these are contracted orders rather than recognised revenue, and no unit-deployment count was disclosed. On a recognised-revenue basis the claim below therefore survives, but it is narrower than it first looks, and Agility is the datapoint most likely to overturn it as the deal proceeds.

Among venture-backed humanoid and robot-foundation-model companies, those with materially disclosed recognised revenue are Chinese. That qualifier matters: the incumbents in A7, A11 and A12 — ABB, FANUC, Caterpillar, Komatsu, John Deere, Symbotic — all disclose substantial robotics and autonomy revenue. The point is not that nobody in the West earns money from robots. It is that within the venture-funded frontier this map is mostly about, the revenue is Chinese and the valuations are not. UBTech led humanoid revenue in 2025 at roughly CNY 2.0B (~$280M), selling 1,079 Walker S2 units for CNY 821M (~$115M) of humanoid-segment revenue. Unitree guided H1 2026 revenue of about CNY 1.1B (~$156M), up 36–45% year on year. AgiBot expects roughly CNY 4.0B (~$594M) for 2026. Global humanoid shipments reached 19,100 units in H1 2026, up 272% year on year TechNode, with AgiBot overtaking Unitree for the top position and the two together holding close to 80% of Chinese output.

Western leaders are valued largely on expected capability. Chinese leaders are valued on units shipped and revenue booked, at roughly 9–25× revenue, which is demanding but within the range familiar from other hardware categories. The Western marks have no denominator to test against. That is not necessarily wrong, since early platform bets often look exactly like this. It does mean the two halves of the map are being underwritten to different evidentiary standards, and only one of them can currently be checked.

Pricing is unsettled even among people who look at the same company

AgiBot's Hong Kong listing target has been reported both as $5.1–6.4B and as HK$156B (roughly $20B), a threefold spread on a transaction that is imminent and disclosed. If the range is that wide on a company about to list, the private marks elsewhere in this map should be read as approximate.

4 · The incumbents occupy less of this map than their position warrants

ABB, FANUC, Yaskawa and KUKA appear only in A7. They hold the installed base: the industry shipped 542,076 industrial robots in 2024 alone, against an operational stock in the millions IFR. They also hold the service revenue, the distribution and the customer relationships that most startups on this map are effectively trying to rent.

They are almost entirely absent from A5, B2 and B5. Historically, industrial-automation incumbents acquire late and expensively rather than build. The Mobileye/Mentee deal suggests the acquisition phase has begun, but it began with a silicon incumbent rather than a robotics one.

The nearer-term pressure on them is not humanoids. Chinese domestic manufacturers outsold foreign suppliers inside China for the first time in 2024, in a market representing 54% of global installations. On that evidence the incumbent question is less about disruption by general-purpose robots later this decade, and more about competitive displacement already underway in their largest single market.

6 · What this map cannot tell you

The gaps that remain, each of which would change one of the conclusions above if it were resolved.

  • Gross margin per deployed robot. Nobody publishes it. Without it, every RaaS and OEM model on this map is a guess about whether the unit works.
  • Exception-handling headcount. The single number that would settle the reliability argument empirically — and its consistent absence from every vendor disclosure is itself informative.
  • Whether B5 is a layer or a feature. If world models collapse into B6 as a training technique rather than a sold product, one of these twenty-five layers disappears.
  • Real depth of the Chinese component tier. Roughly 30 Chinese companies appear here against a population several times larger. In the half of the market that ships the units, this map is thinnest.
  • Renewal and churn in RaaS. Three-year contracts have not yet cycled. The deployment-moat thesis is untested past first renewal.
In physical AI, the same parties increasingly fund, supply and buy from one another. Much of that is deliberate ecosystem construction, and it works. The narrower concern is that when the investor, the supplier and the customer are the same party, a valuation stops being an independent read on what a company is worth. This section separates ecosystem interdependence, which is ordinary and often productive, from demand circularity, which degrades the price signal. They are not the same thing and should not be read as such.

Why ecosystems form here rather than elsewhere

Software markets can bootstrap themselves. A developer ships, users arrive, and revenue follows without much external capital. Physical AI does not work that way. A robot company needs actuators that are not yet made at volume, a simulator it cannot afford to build alone, a factory willing to host an unproven machine, and roughly three years before any of it earns. Each of those depends on somebody else's capital expenditure.

So the participants solve it structurally — a supplier takes equity to justify tooling up, a platform gives away a toolchain to create demand for silicon, an industrial group buys the robot maker outright and deploys into its own plants. Ecosystem construction is the rational response to a market that cannot bootstrap. Intel Capital and Qualcomm Ventures ran the same playbook for decades and built real industries.

The reason to map these relationships is narrower than asking whether the sector is a bubble. It is that when the investor, the supplier and the customer are the same party, the price paid for equity stops being independent evidence. Since valuations are the figures most readily quoted from a map like this one, it is worth knowing which of them were set at arm's length.

When a partnership becomes a financing loop

Across AI generally, roughly $800B of arrangements have been described as circular. Physical AI's version is tighter, because industrial groups can occupy all three roles at once.

1 · The compute loopNVIDIA
The tightest and best documented of the four.
Invests
Equity in Figure (Series B at $2.6B, again in the Series C at $39B), Wayve ($1.05B round, a further ~$500M reported), Skild AI and Field AI.
They build on NVIDIA
Those companies train on Isaac Lab and Cosmos and ship on Jetson, alongside a dozen named ecosystem partners on the same stack.
Revenue returns
Compute and licence spend flows back, and the ecosystem announcements support the platform narrative underwriting NVIDIA's own multiple.
Closing the loopSkild's model — NVIDIA-funded, NVIDIA-trained — runs the Foxconn assembly lines in Houston that build NVIDIA's own Blackwell servers. Investor, toolchain, customer and end market are one company.
Scale: NVIDIA is reported to be working a deal pipeline above $750B, including ~$500B of financing partnerships with BlackRock and Apollo extending credit for GPU purchases. Jensen Huang has publicly rejected the circular-financing characterisation, calling it a virtuous cycle rather than manufactured demand.
2 · The component loopSchaeffler · Bosch
The clearest case of a supplier funding its own demand.
Invests
Schaeffler and Bosch both join Neura Robotics' Series C and Humanoid's Series A. Schaeffler separately partners with Leju Robotics in China.
Supplies them
Schaeffler provides drivetrain components to Agility and to its own portfolio companies — the actuation layer these robots cannot be built without.
Then buys the output
Schaeffler holds an order for 1,000+ robots from Humanoid, reported as implying 100,000 units by 2031, destined for its own plants.
Closing the loopOne company is shareholder, component vendor and anchor customer simultaneously. Each leg is individually rational; together they mean Humanoid's order book and Schaeffler's equity mark are not independent of one another.
In fairness: this is how automotive tier-one supply has always worked — co-development with an equity stake is unremarkable there. The difference is that the robot maker's price is being set in venture markets rather than negotiated as a supply contract.
3 · The conglomerate loopHyundai · Samsung · LG
Where one group owns the maker, the supply chain and the factory.
Owns the maker
Hyundai moved to full ownership of Boston Dynamics via the SoftBank buyout. Samsung raised its Rainbow Robotics stake to ~35% (₩267B) to become largest shareholder. LG took a $60M position in Bear Robotics.
Directs the supply chain
Hyundai has mobilised roughly 200 tier-one suppliers to build a domestic humanoid component base, asking each for a business plan in the category.
Deploys into itself
Hyundai plans about 25,000 Atlas units across its own factories from 2028 — manufacturer, supplier network and customer inside one group.
Closing the loopVertical integration at this scale substitutes internal allocation for a market test. Internal volume demonstrates the group's commitment, which is real information. It does not establish that an unaffiliated buyer would pay the same price.
Why it still counts for something: internal deployment produces real operating hours, and the embodied-data-density argument applies regardless of who the customer is. But an internal transfer price is not a market price.
4 · The strategic syndicateNeura Series C
Less a loop than the absence of an arm's-length buyer.

Neura Robotics' Series C — up to $1.4B, milestone-contingent — was assembled from Tether, NVIDIA, Amazon, Qualcomm, Bosch, Schaeffler, the European Investment Bank and a Qatari sovereign investor. A stablecoin issuer, two chip vendors, a cloud and logistics operator, two component suppliers, a development bank and a sovereign. No classic venture lead set the price.

Reporting on the sector's largest 2025–26 rounds describes the same pattern generally: the biggest cheques were assembled by corporate strategic investors in coordinated syndicates rather than priced by financial investors. Dealroom counted about $8.7B of humanoid venture funding in 2026 to late July.

What this does and does not mean

The comparison critics reach for is dot-com vendor financing: Nortel and Lucent lent customers the money to buy switching gear, and the revenue vanished with the credit. It is a fair analogy for the mechanism and an unfair one for the conclusion — vendor financing did not make fibre worthless, it made the reported demand curve wrong.

So the defensible claim is narrow. Circular structure is not evidence the technology is fake. It is evidence the price signal is degraded. Where investor, supplier and customer are the same party, a headline valuation and an order book corroborate each other rather than independently confirming anything.

Four questions worth asking of any of these relationships

None of these is disqualifying on its own. Together they indicate how much independent evidence sits behind a given valuation or order book.

1Would the customer buy at the same volume absent the equity stake? Schaeffler ordering robots it part-owns is a weaker signal than a Schaeffler competitor ordering them.
2Is the back-flow at market terms and separately disclosed? Ecosystem investment survives scrutiny when the revenue is broken out, and does not when it is buried in a segment line.
3Did a financial investor set the price, or only strategics? A round with no arm's-length lead has no independent pricing anchor.
4Does the company have revenue from outside the loop? This is the most informative of the four, since customers with no other relationship to the company are the clearest evidence that demand is not being manufactured.

What would change this reading

  • A major humanoid or foundation-model company disclosing revenue split by strategic-investor versus independent customer. Nobody currently does, and that absence is itself informative.
  • A priced round led by a financial investor with no commercial relationship to the company, at or above the last strategic mark.
  • Schaeffler's Humanoid order converting to repeat volume from unaffiliated buyers, which would turn an anchor commitment into a market.
  • Conversely, a down round at a company whose last mark was set entirely by its own suppliers and customers would settle the question quickly and publicly.
Scope and limits

This tab maps publicly announced investments, partnerships and orders. Deal terms are mostly undisclosed, so it can establish that a relationship exists but not what was paid or on what terms. Several figures — the NVIDIA pipeline, the additional Wayve tranche, the Neura milestone conditions — are reported rather than confirmed and are described as such above.

Nothing here asserts wrongdoing. Strategic investment is ordinary, frequently value-additive, and in a capital-intensive early market often the only way a supply chain gets built at all. The argument concerns how much a valuation tells you, not anyone's conduct.

Most published landscapes split physical AI into hardware and software, then merge simulation, world models and robot policies into a single bucket. This section sets out why we structure it differently, what that structure makes visible, and where it remains imperfect.

Why not hardware versus software

NVIDIA is silicon and Isaac, Cosmos and GR00T. Figure is hardware and Helix 2. Tesla, Unitree, 1X, Anduril and Waymo are all both. A hardware/software split places the fifteen most important companies in two boxes each, and asserts a separation that does not yet exist in this market. It is not mutually exclusive — and it is not collectively exhaustive either, because it has nowhere to put fleet orchestration, safety certification or systems integration.

The underlying instinct is sound, but it is applied at the wrong level. How hardware-weighted a company is does vary meaningfully across this market. That variation is a gradient running through the layers rather than a dividing line between two halves of the industry.

Three planes, twenty-five layers

Plane A is broadly hardware, Plane B broadly software, and Plane C is the operational layer that is neither — and that determines whether anything ships.

Why B4, B5 and B6 are three layers and not one

They are routinely collapsed into "simulation and models". They are three different technical bets, with different customers and different failure modes.

  • B4 physics simulation solves dynamics from specified equations. You author the world. Failure mode is the sim-to-real gap.
  • B5 world models learn dynamics generatively and predict future states. You sample the world rather than specify it. Failure mode is hallucinated physics — and per NVIDIA's own finding, when the video prediction fails the action fails with it.
  • B6 robot foundation models output motor commands. They consume B4 and B5 output but sell a policy, not an environment.
  • The honest caveat on B5: the standalone commercial market is thin. Several entries — Cosmos, Genie — are internal tooling inside a larger platform rather than products sold on their own, and are tagged internal on the map. Independently funded pure plays do exist (World Labs at $5B, Decart, Odyssey), which is why the layer is kept separate rather than folded away — but it is the layer most likely to collapse into its neighbours.

The assignment rule: unit of sale

Whether a set of categories is genuinely mutually exclusive depends on the rule used to assign things to them, not on how the boxes are drawn. So the rule is stated first. Every company is assigned to one primary layer, its unit of sale: what the customer actually buys. This is deliberately narrower than asking where value is captured, which is too elastic to adjudicate, because it invites an unresolvable debate about where the value really sits. Unit of sale is checkable: Nuro’s customer licenses a driving stack, so Nuro sits in B7; Applied Intuition’s customer buys tooling rather than a model, so B4.

One convention has to be stated outright, because it decides a whole layer. A company that builds its own embodiment and also operates it — selling rides or freight rather than vehicles — is placed at the embodiment layer, with owned-fleet operations recorded on the commercial axis. That is why Waymo, Zoox, Aurora and Pony.ai all sit in A8 rather than scattering into C5. Fourteen of A8’s fifteen entries are operators; filing them away from the machines they run would describe the same business twice and make the layer look emptier than it is.

Secondary positions are recorded as spans and rendered with a dashed border, never as duplicate entries.

Seven companies — Waymo, Tesla, NVIDIA, Anduril, Amazon, Figure, Zoox — are marked full-stack. To be precise about what that does and does not mean: they are still placed at one layer, because the map is a grid and everything has to sit somewhere. The placement is their unit of sale. The mark exists to tell you that the placement is unusually lossy for these seven, and that the spans matter more than the primary layer. It is a warning label on the taxonomy, not an exemption from it.

What Plane C buys you — and what it is not

CB Insights' widely-cited physical AI map covers 70+ companies across 10 categories — all of which sit inside Plane B. It is an excellent map of the intelligence layer, and silent on the other two-thirds of the stack.

Plane C is split for honesty. C1–C3 are genuine technical layers — runtime, fleet operations, certification. C4–C5 are commercial delivery, and they are not technology: systems integration is a low-margin, headcount-heavy professional-services business, and RaaS is an off-balance-sheet financing structure. They earn their place because they own the customer and the operational envelope, but reading them as a software layer would misprice them badly.

That omission matters, because on our reading the binding constraint on industrial deployment is not model capability but certifiability against a documented operational design domain, the specific, bounded set of conditions a system is approved to work within. Roland Berger's four-tier framing of humanoid use cases Roland Berger is the basis for the tiers used here. The reliability rates below are the author's own working assumptions rather than figures from that study, which sets the tiers out qualitatively and publishes no numeric success-rate targets.

The following is an illustration of how sensitive throughput is to error rate, not a measurement. Assume a 60-second takt time, meaning one unit leaves the station each minute, across an 8-hour shift of roughly 450 cycles, with each exception requiring about 4 minutes of human intervention on a coupled line with no buffer. At 98% success that is around 9 interventions and 36 line-minutes, or 7.5% of the shift. At 99.9%, the norm for fixed automation, it is under half an intervention and about 2 minutes, or 0.4%. The gap between those two is roughly nineteenfold in intervention events. C3 is the layer where that gap is closed, and it is among the least mapped in the industry.

Sensitivity, because the 19× depends on two choices I made: whether the assumed “>98%” is read as per-cycle task success or as system uptime, and whether the line is coupled with no buffer. Change the second and the conclusion softens considerably. In a buffered parallel cell — independent stations, work-in-progress between them, a failed unit simply pulled from rotation — the same 9 exceptions per shift cost throughput roughly in proportion to the failure rate rather than stopping the line, so the penalty falls from ~7.5% of shift time to something closer to 2%. That is the architecture where cheap redundancy substitutes for reliability, and it is why logistics adopted robots a decade before manufacturing did. The reliability argument is an argument about coupled linear lines specifically, not about robots in general.

Both the reliability rates and the takt assumptions above are the author's own, chosen to illustrate sensitivity. Roland Berger is the source for the four-tier structure only; that study publishes no numeric reliability targets. No figure in this paragraph is a sourced one.

The commercial axis: how value is captured

There is a fair objection to everything above. A map organised around where value is captured, but which assigns every company to exactly one technical layer, is answering two questions with one structure. Waymo and Anduril capture value partly by spanning layers, and C4 and C5 are commercial structures in a technology layer's clothing. Marking the difficult cases full-stack and moving on would not resolve that.

The resolution is that the map reads on two axes rather than one. The layer says what the customer buys. The business model says how the seller gets paid. Eight values: component supply, machine sale, software licence, robots-as-a-service, owned-fleet operations, integration and manufacturing, testing and certification, open source. Every entry on the map carries both, and the pair is cross-tabulated in the Layer × model view under Explore.

The two axes turn out to be correlated without being identical, and both halves of that matter. Twelve of the twenty-five layers are a single business model. Every company in A1 through A5 sells components; every company in A6 sells machines; B1, B5, B7, B8 and C2 are wholly software. Where a layer is a monoculture the layer alone is a sufficient description, and the objection has no force there.

Where the axes diverge is where the second one earns its place. A8 is barely a platform layer at all: fourteen of its fifteen companies are owned-fleet operators who never sell a vehicle to anyone. That is by convention rather than accident, but it means the layer is named for its machines while its economics are entirely services. B2 splits four software businesses against five services businesses, a margin distinction the layer hides completely. And full-stack turns out not to be one phenomenon but three. Waymo and Zoox span layers because an operator has to own its whole stack in order to sell the outcome. Figure, Tesla, Amazon and Anduril span for cost and capability while still selling a machine. NVIDIA spans by selling components into every layer at once. One tag, three unrelated economic reasons, legible only on the commercial axis.

The clearest single reading on the grid is A6. Twenty-six humanoid companies, all twenty-six selling the same thing in the same way. No cell on this map is more crowded on both axes at once. That concentration is the commoditisation argument from the Value capture section, visible in a single square.

This does not rescue C4 and C5, and it is not meant to. Those two remain the layers where the axes very nearly coincide, kept as layers because the customer's procurement genuinely differs: you buy integration hours, or you buy robot-hours, and in both cases the commercial structure is the unit of sale. That is the one place where the taxonomy is answering two questions with one box, and it is stated here rather than hidden.

The tier framework's hardest test — and why it survives it

Mining and agriculture appear to falsify this table. Caterpillar's MineStar and Komatsu's FrontRunner have run driverless haul trucks in production mines for over a decade, moving billions of tonnes in open, unstructured terrain. John Deere ships a fully autonomous tractor. That looks like Tier 3–4 autonomy, profitable, today.

It is not a contradiction — it is the strongest available evidence for the argument. A mine haul road is geofenced, single-task, low-speed, on private land with no public-road regulator and no member of the public present. A field is a bounded polygon. These are not general-purpose systems operating in the open world; they are narrow, rigorously documented operational design domains, which is precisely the condition this map argues you need. The lesson is not that Tier 4 arrived early. It is that when you constrain the envelope hard enough, autonomy has been commercially viable in heavy industry for fifteen years — and that is the template, not the humanoid.

Read the two axes together and the map's central tension appears: capital is concentrated in Plane B and Tier 3–4 ambition, while revenue is concentrated in Plane A/C and Tier 1–2 reality — plus the mining and agriculture deployments that most physical AI maps leave out entirely.

How the map was built, what it excludes, and where the data is weakest. Set out in full so that a reader can judge how much weight each part of it will bear.

Taxonomy rules

  1. One primary layer per entry, assigned by unit of sale — what the customer buys — not by what the company touches, aspires to, or is valued on.
  2. Product lines can be their own entry where they compete as separate products against separate rivals. Isaac Sim, Cosmos, GR00T and Halos are listed individually because a simulation buyer, a world-model buyer and a certification buyer are different people running different procurements. Those entries are tagged product, excluded from the company count, and removed from the parent's spans so nothing is counted twice. 243 distinct companies, 251 entries.
  3. Ties broken by what the customer buys. Applied Intuition touches models but sells tooling, so B4. Symbotic builds robots but sells an integrated warehouse, so A7 with a span into C4 and C5.
  4. Secondary positions recorded as spans, rendered as dashed borders, never as duplicate entries. Where no layer dominates, the company is marked full-stack instead of being forced.
  5. Geography is headquarters, not manufacturing or market. Several Western OEMs manufacture predominantly in China; that is a supply-chain fact, not a taxonomy fact.
  6. Verticals are tags, not boxes. A company can serve several. Only its layer is exclusive.

Valuation methodology

  • Valuations only — never round sizes. A $1.4B round and a $14B valuation are different facts, and showing both in one column invites misreading.
  • Publicly listed companies show no figure, only a listed tag. Market caps move daily and would date the map within a week.
  • Reported-but-unclosed marks are flagged in amber. 1X at ~$10B and AgiBot at $5.1–6.4B are talks and IPO targets, not completed rounds. Physical Intelligence is shown at its confirmed $5.6B, not the ~$11B reported in negotiation.
  • Blank means undisclosed, not small. PitchBook shows no post-money for Waabi despite a $750M round — a disclosure gap, not an absence of value.
  • Headline marks are not market caps, and should not be read as one. Late-stage private valuations are pre-money headline numbers agreed between a company and a small number of investors. They routinely sit on top of liquidation preferences, participation rights and ratchets that mean common equity is worth materially less than the headline implies. Several rounds on this map also involve strategic or circular structure — compute commitments, supplier equity, or an infrastructure vendor investing in a customer that buys its hardware. Aggregating these figures overstates the sector's real market scale, and does so most in Plane B, where marks are highest and revenue is thinnest.

Business model, maturity and deployments

Three fields added in V5. The first two are classifications the author assigned; the third is compiled from public announcements. None are sourced financial figures and none should be read as such.

  • Business model is the unit of sale restated commercially — what the customer buys and how they pay for it. Eight values: component supply, machine sale, software licence, robots-as-a-service, owned-fleet operations, integration & manufacturing, testing & certification, open source. Derived from the primary layer, then overridden by hand where the layer misleads: Waymo sits in A8 but sells rides rather than vehicles, so owned-fleet operations; Locus builds robots but bills per unit per month, so RaaS.
  • For pre-commercial companies the business model is intended, not observed. A little over a quarter of the entries on this map are marked research or pilot, which includes several of the most highly valued names in it. For those companies the recorded business model describes how they say they will charge, not a mechanism that has been tested against paying customers. The commercial axis is therefore a statement of intent in the upper layers of Plane B and much of A6, and a description of observed practice in A1–A5, A7, A11, A12 and Plane C. Read it accordingly, and use the maturity field alongside it rather than on its own.
  • Commercial maturity describes the primary layer, not the company. Four stages: research, pilot, commercial, scaled. The distinction matters for the vertically integrated. Tesla is marked pilot because its mapped layer is A6 humanoid platforms and Optimus is a pilot — not because Tesla is an early-stage company.
  • Deployments are publicly reported only — company announcements and press coverage, neither confirmed with the companies nor audited. 71 of 251 entries carry one.
  • Absence is a research gap, not a finding. A company with no deployment listed has not been researched for one, and its profile says so rather than showing an empty field. An empty field reads as evidence that none exist; it is only evidence that none were checked.
  • Revenue is deliberately not a field. It is not reliably available for private companies at this scale, and a column populated by estimates would be the least defensible thing on the map. The screening table says this in place of the column.

How this differs from the published maps

a16z, CB Insights and the VC landscape posts are organised by category membership — useful for discovery, and silent on where value goes. This map is organised by unit of sale, states its assignment rule, extends beyond the intelligence layer into operations, and attaches a falsifiable view about margin geography.

Being precise about what is and is not original here, because a reader who works it out unaided will discount more than the truth warrants: the company roster is compiled, not discovered. The Capital tab is substantially a restatement of one Crunchbase analysis plus PitchBook round medians — accurate, verified, and not mine.
