On this page

How value becomes profit.

A useful product is not automatically a strong business. Connect the customer’s outcome to the supplier’s competitive position and the economics of delivering it.

How to use it on the map

Pick a company and a specific customer offer. Establish the task value, compare replacement options, then examine delivery costs. A layer identifies what is sold; a business model identifies how it is paid for. Neither establishes pricing power or profitability.

Example: fleet-operations software

For a hypothetical provider in C2, apply the tests to one customer offer:

  • Customer value: compare recovery time and supervision effort with and without the software.
  • Competitive position: assess the cost of migrating workflows, integrations and operating history. An API alone is not a switching cost; a sustainably lower delivery cost can also be an advantage.
  • Delivery economics: compare fees with integration, support and any human supervision the provider supplies.

The example is illustrative. Evidence must come from actual deployments, customer alternatives and company costs.

Follow the revenue after installation

Intuitive Surgical shows why sales beyond the system deserve attention. Its teleoperated surgical systems are an adjacent comparison, outside this map’s autonomy scope. The mix does not establish the same economics for autonomous robots.

Revenue category is not contract type

The Systems line includes lease revenue. The 75.4% share outside Systems is therefore not a measure of all recurring revenue. This chart also does not show margins by category.

What would weaken a value-capture thesis?

A cheaper substitute meets the same requirement

Compare task outcomes and total customer cost, including migration and revalidation. A claimed bottleneck needs evidence that substitution is difficult.

Deployment volume increases support costs as fast as fees

Track delivery cost per deployed unit or operating hour. More recurring revenue is useful only if the economics of serving it hold up.

The claimed data advantage cannot be used

Check ownership, permission, relevance and demonstrated performance gains. Capturing data is not the same as owning a useful advantage.

Use customer value, substitution and delivery costs together. Evidence of demand, revenue mix and profitability answer different questions.

Turn a market thesis into a testable question

Components & assurance

Does qualification create an expensive-to-replace dependency?

Disconfirmation: substitution with equivalent performance and little revalidation cost.
Data & model businesses

Does the provider own useful data rights or differentiated capability?

Disconfirmation: public data or open models meet the same requirements.
Operating models

Do recurring payments cover hardware, service, supervision and capital costs?

Disconfirmation: utilization or support costs erase the margin.
General-purpose platforms

Can a broader machine beat a simpler alternative at the customer’s required task?

Disconfirmation: customization and recovery costs persist across deployments.

Company-level costs, retention and intervention requirements remain the key evidence gaps. Use these questions to guide targeted company research.

The map follows the purchase.

A company’s primary layer describes the offer a customer buys. Its business model describes how the seller gets paid.

Why not hardware versus software

NVIDIA is silicon and Isaac, Cosmos and GR00T. Figure is hardware and Helix 2. Tesla, Unitree, 1X, Anduril and Waymo are all both. A hardware/software split duplicates integrated companies and obscures fleet operations, safety assessment and systems integration. A purchase-based taxonomy makes the buyer and competing offer easier to identify.

The underlying instinct is sound, but it is applied at the wrong level. How hardware-weighted a company is does vary meaningfully across this market. That variation is a gradient running through the layers rather than a dividing line between two halves of the industry.

Three planes, twenty-five layers

Body covers components and machines. Brain covers data and autonomy. Operations covers deployment software, assurance and commercial delivery. A company has one primary layer and may span others.

Why B4, B5 and B6 are three layers and not one

They are routinely collapsed into "simulation and models". They are three different technical bets, with different customers and different failure modes.

  • B4 physics simulation solves dynamics from specified equations. You author the world. Failure mode is the sim-to-real gap.
  • B5 world models learn dynamics generatively and predict future states. You sample the world rather than specify it. Failure mode is hallucinated physics — and per NVIDIA's own finding, when the video prediction fails the action fails with it.
  • B6 robot foundation models output motor commands. They consume B4 and B5 output but sell a policy, not an environment.
  • The honest caveat on B5: the standalone commercial market is thin. Several entries — Cosmos, Genie — are internal tooling inside a larger platform rather than products sold on their own, and are tagged internal on the map. Independently funded pure plays do exist (World Labs, Decart and Odyssey; AMD has announced a pending acquisition of World Labs), which is why the layer is kept separate rather than folded away — its standalone commercial role is a hypothesis to test against paid demand, differentiation and platform alternatives.

The assignment rule: unit of sale

Whether a set of categories is genuinely mutually exclusive depends on the rule used to assign things to them, not on how the boxes are drawn. So the rule is stated first. Every company is assigned to one primary layer, its unit of sale: what the customer actually buys. This is deliberately narrower than asking where value is captured, which is too elastic to adjudicate, because it invites an unresolvable debate about where the value really sits. Unit of sale is checkable: Nuro’s customer licenses a driving stack, so Nuro sits in B7; Applied Intuition’s customer buys tooling rather than a model, so B4.

One convention has to be stated outright, because it decides a whole layer. A company that builds its own embodiment and also operates it — selling rides or freight rather than vehicles — is placed at the embodiment layer, with owned-fleet operations recorded on the commercial axis. That is why Waymo, Zoox, Aurora and Pony.ai all sit in A8 rather than scattering into C5. Fourteen of A8’s fifteen entries are operators; filing them away from the machines they run would describe the same business twice and make the layer look emptier than it is.

Secondary positions are recorded as spans and rendered with a dashed border, never as duplicate entries.

Seven companies — Waymo, Tesla, NVIDIA, Anduril, Amazon, Figure, Zoox — are marked full-stack. To be precise about what that does and does not mean: they are still placed at one layer, because the map is a grid and everything has to sit somewhere. The placement is their unit of sale. The mark exists to tell you that the placement is unusually lossy for these seven, and that the spans matter more than the primary layer. It is a warning label on the taxonomy, not an exemption from it.

Deployment operations and reliability

An intelligence-focused landscape can leave out components, whole machines and deployment operations. This map includes those purchase categories so buyers can examine the full delivery chain.

Plane C is split for honesty. C1–C3 are genuine technical layers — runtime, fleet operations, certification. C4–C5 are commercial delivery, and they are not technology: integration involves project delivery and support, while RaaS packages access or outcomes through ongoing payments. Margins and accounting treatment depend on the contract and on who owns, finances and operates the equipment. They earn their place because they own the customer and the operational envelope, but their economics require delivery-cost and contract evidence, not a software-margin assumption.

That omission matters, because deployment requires model capability, reliable operations and assurance within a defined operating domain, the bounded conditions the system is designed to handle. Which constraint dominates depends on the task and customer requirements. Roland Berger's four-tier framing of humanoid use cases Roland Berger is the basis for the tiers used here. The reliability rates below are the author's own working assumptions rather than figures from that study, which sets the tiers out qualitatively and publishes no numeric success-rate targets.

The following is an illustration of how sensitive throughput is to error rate, not a measurement. Assume a 60-second takt time, meaning one unit leaves the station each minute, across an 8-hour shift of roughly 450 cycles, with each exception requiring about 4 minutes of human intervention on a coupled line with no buffer. At 98% success that is around 9 interventions and 36 line-minutes, or 7.5% of the shift. At 99.9% per-cycle success, it is under half an intervention and about 2 minutes, or 0.4%. The gap between those two is twentyfold in intervention events. C3 is the layer where that gap is closed, and it is among the least mapped in the industry.

Sensitivity, because the 20× ratio depends on two choices I made: whether the assumed “>98%” is read as per-cycle task success or as system uptime, and whether the line is coupled with no buffer. Change the second and the conclusion softens considerably. In a buffered parallel cell — independent stations, work-in-progress between them, a failed unit simply pulled from rotation — the same 9 exceptions per shift cost throughput roughly in proportion to the failure rate rather than stopping the line, so the penalty falls from ~7.5% of shift time to something closer to 2%. That is the architecture where cheap redundancy substitutes for reliability, and it is why logistics adopted robots a decade before manufacturing did. The reliability argument is an argument about coupled linear lines specifically, not about robots in general.

Both the reliability rates and the takt assumptions above are the author's own, chosen to illustrate sensitivity. Roland Berger is the source for the four-tier structure only; that study publishes no numeric reliability targets. No figure in this paragraph is a sourced one.

The commercial axis: how value is captured

There is a fair objection to everything above. A map organized around where value is captured, but which assigns every company to exactly one technical layer, is answering two questions with one structure. Waymo and Anduril capture value partly by spanning layers, and C4 and C5 are commercial structures in a technology layer's clothing. Marking the difficult cases full-stack and moving on would not resolve that.

The resolution is that the map reads on two axes rather than one. The layer says what the customer buys. The business model says how the seller gets paid. Eight values: component supply, machine sale, software license, robots-as-a-service, owned-fleet operations, integration and manufacturing, testing and certification, open source. Every entry on the map carries both, and the pair is cross-tabulated in the Layer × model view under Explore.

The two axes turn out to be correlated without being identical, and both halves of that matter. Twelve of the twenty-five layers are a single business model. Every company in A1 through A5 sells components; every company in A6 sells machines; B1, B5, B7, B8 and C2 are wholly software. Where a layer is a monoculture the layer alone is a sufficient description, and the objection has no force there.

Where the axes diverge is where the second one earns its place. A8 is barely a platform layer at all: fourteen of its fifteen companies are owned-fleet operators who never sell a vehicle to anyone. That is by convention rather than accident, but it means the layer is named for its machines while its economics are entirely services. B2 splits four software businesses against five services businesses, a margin distinction the layer hides completely. And full-stack turns out not to be one phenomenon but three. Waymo and Zoox span layers because an operator has to own its whole stack in order to sell the outcome. Figure, Tesla, Amazon and Anduril span for cost and capability while still selling a machine. NVIDIA spans by selling components into every layer at once. One tag, three unrelated economic reasons, legible only on the commercial axis.

The clearest single reading on the grid is A6. 24 humanoid-platform entries, all classified here as machine sales. This is a crowded layer/model intersection. The shared classification does not imply identical pricing, service terms or capabilities. That concentration is the commoditization argument from the Value capture section, visible in a single square.

This does not rescue C4 and C5, and it is not meant to. Those two remain the layers where the axes very nearly coincide, kept as layers because the customer's procurement genuinely differs: you buy integration hours, or you buy robot-hours, and in both cases the commercial structure is the unit of sale. That is the one place where the taxonomy is answering two questions with one box, and it is stated here rather than hidden.

The tier framework's hardest test — and why it survives it

Mining and agriculture appear to falsify this table. Caterpillar's MineStar and Komatsu's FrontRunner have run driverless haul trucks in production mines for over a decade, moving billions of tonnes in open, unstructured terrain. John Deere ships a fully autonomous tractor. That looks like Tier 3–4 autonomy, profitable, today.

It is not a contradiction — it is the strongest available evidence for the argument. A mine haul road is geofenced, single-task, low-speed, on private land with no public-road regulator and no member of the public present. A field is a bounded polygon. These are not general-purpose systems operating in the open world; they are narrow, rigorously documented operational design domains, which is precisely the condition this map argues you need. The lesson is not that Tier 4 arrived early. It is that when you constrain the envelope hard enough, autonomy has been commercially viable in heavy industry for fifteen years — and that is the template, not the humanoid.

A useful commercial tension is that investors fund broad future capability while buyers contract for bounded operational outcomes. Read the layer, business model and maturity together. This roster does not establish plane-level revenue or capital totals.

The rules behind the map.

Coverage, classifications, dates and evidence limits are explicit so the reader can challenge the assignments.

Technical terms used in the map

ODD (operational design domain): the conditions and task boundaries within which an autonomous system is intended to operate. VLA (vision-language-action): a model that uses visual observations and language to generate robot actions. AMR: autonomous mobile robot. RaaS: robots as a service; the provider's ownership, financing and support obligations depend on the contract.

Takt time: the production pace required to meet demand. Teleoperation: direct remote human control, distinct from autonomous execution. Inspection and certification: different assurance activities; a component assessment or platform report does not certify a complete deployment. All dollar-denominated amounts are USD unless another currency is explicitly stated.

Taxonomy rules

  1. One primary layer per entry, assigned by unit of sale — what the customer buys — not by what the company touches, aspires to, or is valued on.
  2. Product lines can be their own entry where they compete as separate products against separate rivals. Isaac Sim, Cosmos, GR00T and Halos are listed individually because a simulation buyer, a world-model buyer and a certification buyer are different people running different procurements. Those entries are tagged product, excluded from the company count, and removed from the parent's spans so nothing is counted twice. 244 distinct companies, 252 entries.
  3. Ties broken by what the customer buys. Applied Intuition touches models but sells tooling, so B4. Symbotic builds robots but sells an integrated warehouse, so A7 with a span into C4 and C5.
  4. Secondary positions recorded as spans, rendered as dashed borders, never as duplicate entries. Where no layer dominates, the company is marked full-stack instead of being forced.
  5. Geography is headquarters, not manufacturing or market. Several Western OEMs manufacture predominantly in China; that is a supply-chain fact, not a taxonomy fact.
  6. Verticals are tags, not boxes. A company can serve several. Only its layer is exclusive.

Valuation methodology

  • Valuations only — never round sizes. A $1.4B round and a $14B valuation are different facts, and showing both in one column invites misreading.
  • Publicly listed companies show no figure, only a listed tag. Market caps move daily and would date the map within a week.
  • Reported, estimated and unclosed marks are flagged in amber; amber does not always mean the round remains open. 1X at approximately USD 6B is an acquisition rumor; World Labs at approximately USD 8.2B is an announced acquisition; AgiBot at USD 5.1–6.4B is an IPO target. None is a completed equity round. Physical Intelligence is shown at PitchBook’s USD 11.7B completed May 2026 round.
  • Blank means undisclosed, not small. PitchBook shows no post-money for Waabi despite a $750M round — a disclosure gap, not an absence of value.
  • Headline marks are not public market caps or common-equity values. Disclosed private marks can be pre- or post-money; transaction type and basis are stated where known. Preferences, participation rights and other terms can change the value attributable to common shares. Strategic commitments may also affect pricing. Do not sum these different instruments into an estimate of sector market size.

Business model, maturity and deployments

Three fields added in V5. The first two are classifications the author assigned; the third is compiled from public announcements. None are sourced financial figures and none should be read as such.

  • Business model is the unit of sale restated commercially — what the customer buys and how they pay for it. Eight values: component supply, machine sale, software license, robots-as-a-service, owned-fleet operations, integration & manufacturing, testing & certification, open source. Derived from the primary layer, then overridden by hand where the layer misleads: Waymo sits in A8 but sells rides rather than vehicles, so owned-fleet operations; Locus builds robots but bills per unit per month, so RaaS.
  • For pre-commercial companies the business model is intended, not observed. A little over a quarter of the entries on this map are marked research or pilot, which includes several of the most highly valued names in it. For those companies the recorded business model describes how they say they will charge, not a mechanism that has been tested against paying customers. The commercial axis is therefore a statement of intent in the upper layers of Plane B and much of A6, and a description of observed practice in A1–A5, A7, A11, A12 and Plane C. Read it accordingly, and use the maturity field alongside it rather than on its own.
  • Commercial maturity describes the primary layer, not the company. Four stages: research, pilot, commercial, scaled. The distinction matters for the vertically integrated. Tesla is marked pilot because its mapped layer is A6 humanoid platforms and Optimus is a pilot — not because Tesla is an early-stage company.
  • Deployments are publicly reported only — company announcements and press coverage, neither confirmed with the companies nor audited. 73 of 252 entries carry a named deployment or program. This includes pilots, collaborations and announced programs; it is not a count of production installations.
  • Absence is a research gap, not a finding. A company with no deployment listed has not been researched for one, and its profile says so rather than showing an empty field. An empty field reads as evidence that none exist; it is only evidence that none were checked.
  • Revenue is deliberately not a field. It is not reliably available for private companies at this scale, and a column populated by estimates would be the least defensible thing on the map. The screening table says this in place of the column.

Known weaknesses

GapEffect
China under-mappedThe most consequential gap. China ships the majority of both humanoid and industrial units, but English-language coverage of its component and integrator tiers is thin. Roughly 30 Chinese companies appear here against a real population several times larger.
Valuation stalenessPrivate marks are disclosed irregularly. In a market repricing this fast, several entries may understate by a full round.
Conflicting sourcesSaronic is reported at both $9.25B and ~$4B; the Crunchbase Series D figure is used. Apptronik's round and valuation figures conflict across trackers; ~$5.3B is the most consistently reported. Helsing appears at both $12B and $18B; the confirmed lower figure is used.
Plane C under-populatedPartly real — the layer is genuinely immature — and partly a coverage gap, since integrators and RaaS operators are often private, unbranded, or embedded inside larger service businesses.
Deployment coverage is partial73 of 252 entries carry a named deployment or program, including pilots and announcements. The other 179 were not researched for one, so the field is a floor on activity rather than a measure of it — and it skews toward companies that publicise, which favours US and European startups over Chinese and Japanese incumbents.
Maturity is a judgementThe four stages are assigned by the author from public positioning, not from revenue disclosure. The commercial/scaled boundary in particular is a reading, not a threshold, and a stricter reader would move perhaps a dozen companies down a stage.
SurvivorshipWound-down and acquired companies (Cruise, Covariant, Kindred) are largely absent, which flatters the sector's apparent success rate.
Full-stack is a judgementSeven companies are marked full-stack. That line is drawn by whether any single layer plausibly accounts for the majority of the offer — defensible, but not mechanical. A stricter reading would mark more; a looser one, fewer.
Incumbent depth in A10Mining and agriculture are represented by their major OEMs and a handful of retrofit startups. The tier-two supplier base in both — and the service contractors who actually run the fleets — is not mapped.

How this differs from the published maps

a16z, CB Insights and the VC landscape posts are organized by category membership — useful for discovery, and silent on where value goes. This map is organized by unit of sale, states its assignment rule, extends beyond the intelligence layer into operations, and attaches a falsifiable view about margin geography.

The company roster is compiled from public research and financial sources. The Capital chart reuses a cited Crunchbase dataset; the concentration ratios are calculated here. This map’s contribution is the buyer-based assignment rule, a separate commercial axis, explicit deployment limits and testable interpretations of where pricing power could persist.
